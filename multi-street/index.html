Multi-Street Games

Chapters 14 & 19 of Chen & Ankenman's The Mathematics of Poker: what size to bet when the bet size is yours to choose — on one street, on many, and in the limit of infinitely many. Home of the book's most quoted result, the geometric growth of pot.

One street first: the value of a bet size (ch. 14)

Give the bettor a polarized range (nuts or air) and charge him bet s (in pots). His bluffs are priced by α = s/(1+s) and his value function f(s) does the rest:

Clairvoyance game: f(s) = ys/(1+s) rises forever — jam. The AKQ game: f(s) = s(1−s)/(6(1+s)) peaks at s* = √2−1 ≈ 0.414 — the "golden mean of poker," r. (A quiet oddity: at s*, X's calling frequency with Kings, (1−r)/(1+r), is also exactly r.) In the [0,1] version Y bets a different amount with every hand — value function y(s) = 3/(7(1+s)²), call function x(s) = 6/(7(1+s)), bluff mass exactly 1/7.

The geometric growth of pot (ch. 19)

Static hands, clairvoyant bettor, M streets, stacks S pots deep. Each street the non-clairvoyant player simply folds α and calls; the bettor stacks bluffs street over street. The optimal sizing grows the pot by the same ratio G = (1+2S)1/M every street, landing all-in on the river.

Why it matters: the $185 case study (Example 19.3)

The same stack, bet in the same number of streets — and the sizing scheme alone swings the game from losing to winning. The small first bet is not timidity: it buys a 46% call-through rate at every step of a pot that is growing 3.33× per street.

Infinite streets: the auction game (Examples 19.4–19.6)

Let the number of streets go to infinity and betting becomes an auction: each hand silently commits to a total s it is willing to put in. The equilibrium commitment curves are square roots:

x(s) = 1/√(1+2s) — the caller's hands committing at least s; y(s) = y·√((1+2S)/(1+2s)) (capped at 1) — the clairvoyant's. The M-street geometric game converges to these curves as M→∞ (verified to 2×10⁻³ at M=1000 in solve.py), and position's value goes to zero — the auction is symmetric.
