Risk

Chapters 22–25 of Chen & Ankenman's The Mathematics of Poker: staying in action. Risk of ruin as an exponential law, what happens when you admit you don't know your own win rate, the Kelly criterion for choosing games, and poker as finance — swaps, Sharpe ratios, and backing agreements priced as options.

Risk of ruin is an exponential (ch. 22)

From one axiom — R(a+b) = R(a)·R(b), losing two stacked bankrolls means losing each — ruin probability must be R(b) = e−αb. The constant α solves ⟨e−αx⟩ = 1 over the game's outcome distribution; for (approximately) normal outcomes, α = 2μ/σ².

Ruin probability against bankroll (bets). Dots mark successive half-bankrolls b½ = ln2/α — each one halves the risk, like half-lives of a radioactive sample. Caveats the book is emphatic about: skim profit off the top and your true R is 100%; tournament distributions are too skew for the normal formula (their direct α gives roughly half the normal approximation's ruin probability in the winner-take-all example).

Risk of ruin when you don't know your win rate (ch. 23)

Your observed win rate after N hands is itself a random variable with standard error σw = s/√N. Weighting the ruin probability over that uncertainty gives the closed form RoRU = R(w,b)·e2b²σw²/σ⁴·Φ((w−2bσw²/σ²)/σw) + Φ(−w/σw) — the last term is the blunt possibility that you're a losing player and no bankroll saves you.

RoRU (orange) vs naive RoR (blue) as a function of win rate, at your chosen N and bankroll (σ fixed at 18 bets/100 — the book's case study). The gap is the price of uncertainty. The book's going-pro example sits at the dots: 40,000 hands, w = 2.5 bets/100, 300-bet roll → RoR 0.98% but RoRU 3.67%; deduct 1 bet/100 for living expenses and it's 6.22% vs 17.25%. Taking a salary out of a thin edge is how pros go broke. The asymmetry is the lesson: overestimating your win rate slightly understates your risk a lot.

Log-utility growth per hour, U ≈ μ/B − (μ²+σ²)/2B², for both games; the crossing is the cutoff 2c = μ₁+μ₂+(σ₁²−σ₂²)/(μ₁−μ₂). At the book's own numbers ($35/$400 vs $60/$800) the derivation correctly reaches 2c = $19,295 — and then prints c = $19,295; the cutoff is c = $9,647.50, confirmed here by the utility crossover. Kelly sizing itself: a 3% even-money edge bets 3% of bankroll ($150 of $5,000 — Example 24.1, reproduced).

A standard backing deal — backer eats all losses, profits split — is a call option at strike 0: value of the wins = (σ/√2π)e−(a−w)²/2σ² + w·Φ((w−a)/σ). The book's example (w=$4,000, σ=$6,000): wins worth $4,906.72, so the 50/50 player takes $2,453.36 — 61.3% of the whole deal's EV (the book's backer figure, $1,547.64, is a $1 slip for $1,546.64). Swaps: trading 36% of yourself with an equal-EV player at 9-vs-12-buyin variance maximizes your Sharpe ratio (a₁ = σ₂²/(σ₂²+σ₁²) = 0.64). And diversifying across game types isn't diversification at all — you only ever hold one game at a time.
